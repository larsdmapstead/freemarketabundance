A program for a free, abundant America by 2040

Abundance is produced.

Rent eats your paycheck. The power bill keeps climbing. The doctor can see you in March. Every fix on offer is another program, another permit, another fee.

Now picture 2040. You own your home. Your power bill is small enough to ignore. Your doctor posts prices and sees you this week. You changed careers twice, and both times the door was open. Nobody handed you this. Millions of people built it, because the rules finally let them.

Every promise here has a receipt.

What would you build?

What disappears

The things that make life expensive and slow today. In 2040, they are gone.

  • The waiting listMore clinics and nurses are allowed to treat you.
  • The permit delayA home that meets the code gets built, on a deadline.
  • The licensing feeYour license works in every state, and work that hurts nobody needs none.
  • The surprise billEvery price is posted before you are treated.
  • Rationed powerFinished solar, storage and reactors are allowed to plug in.
  • Twenty years of student debtYou learn a trade while you get paid for it.
  • The tariff at checkoutYou buy from anyone, anywhere, at the real price.

Who is in the way

Not you. Scarcity is made by permission: the permit office that sits on a new building for years, the license board that charges you to do work you already know how to do, the rule that protects the big company from the small one. Every shortage you feel has an address. We are going to close it.

That is the promise. Here is why it is the one that gets kept.

Free people build it.

Homes, health care, energy. Someone has to build the house, train the nurse and run the power plant. The system that wins is the one that gets the most people building, and nothing gets people building like freedom.

Free people produce more. When you can start a business without permission, keep what you earn, trade with anyone and change jobs when you want, you have every reason to work hard, try something new and get better at it. Millions of people doing that at once is what makes things cheap.

People under systems that decide for them produce less. When a plan sets your job, your price and your output, there is no reward for doing better and no way to try a better idea without approval. The plan stalls, the shelves empty, and the people who could have fixed it are not allowed to.

The record

Rank the world’s countries by economic freedom and the gap between the top and the bottom is enormous, in income and in years of life.

That pattern is not one study. A review of 198 studies using that index found that over two thirds tied economic freedom to good outcomes, such as faster growth, and fewer than 4 percent tied it to bad ones. The direction has held across decades and continents.

It shows in how people feel about their lives, too. Across countries, freedom to make life choices is one of six factors that together account for more than three quarters of the differences in how people rate their lives.

It is already happening.

Since 1990, the share of humanity living in extreme poverty fell from 38 percent to under 9 percent. Measured in hours of work, the basics cost 70.9 percent less than in 1980. Solar power got 99.9 percent cheaper. Nobody handed any of it out. Free people produced it.

Now imagine what they build when we stop getting in their way.

How we get there

Ten points. Every one removes a rule that stops people from producing: building homes, opening clinics, connecting power plants, learning trades, moving for work and starting companies. No new bureaucracy. Government gets out of the way, and people do the rest.

Our perspective

Every promise above has a builder, a price that fell, and a rule that got out of the way. This is what we believe.

  • You are the starting point

    People do not exist to serve a collective. Freedom means the ability to own, create, exchange, associate, dissent, and pursue your own version of a good life.

  • Cooperation is voluntary, and it is everywhere

    Firms, cooperatives, charities, congregations, mutual aid societies and neighborhood funds are all free people choosing to work together. None of them needs a mandate.

  • Capital is tools

    Laboratories, factories, computing, savings and investment are how work gets easier. Every dollar taxed or tied up in red tape is a tool that never gets built.

  • Prices are information

    Competition and price signals coordinate knowledge no planner can collect. Where prices are hidden or fixed, the information dies and the shortages start.

  • Abundance is produced, not allocated

    Cheaper intelligence speeds discovery. Cheaper energy makes everything cheaper to make. Robotics cuts the labor in every good. Competition passes the gains to you.

  • Builders and workers, together

    Breakthroughs come from founders and from the engineers, suppliers, workers and customers around them. Extraordinary achievement and broad gains are the same story.

  • A government you barely notice

    Rights, impartial courts, contracts and defense. Small enough to stay out of your way, strong enough to stop violence, theft and fraud.

  • Flourishing is more than GDP

    Health, family, meaning, creativity, time and community. A cheaper life is the floor. What you do with the freedom is the point.

What got cheaper since 2000

Televisions, phones, software, clothing, toys. The things where anyone can enter, compete and sell.

What got pricier since 2000

Hospital care, college, childcare, housing. The things that are licensed, subsidized and supply-restricted. The complaint is right. The cure is supply.

What we fight for

Ten points. Each one names the rule that changes. None needs a new tax, a new agency, a subsidy, or a government stake in a private company. Open any point to see how it works.

  1. 1

    More time, more opportunity

    See how it works

    Productivity is how people win back their time. Open every job to people who can do it, degree or not. Stop cheap federal loans from inflating tuition. Let new schools, apprenticeships and skills tests compete. Learning should cost less every year.

    What is broken

    More than one in five working Americans holds a government license to do their job, and a license earned in one state often counts for nothing in the next. Meanwhile the main ticket to a good job, a degree, keeps getting more expensive, and federal loans help push the price up.

    How we fix it

    • Hire on tested skill, not on a degree the work does not need, starting with every public job.
    • Honor a license earned in one state in every state.
    • Put a sunset on every license: prove it prevents real harm, or it expires.
    • Cap federal lending so it stops feeding tuition increases.
    • Give apprenticeships, skills tests and new schools the same access to students and credit that colleges get.

    The average American worker put in 3,096 hours a year in 1870 and 1,789 in 2023, about 42 percent fewer. No law mandated it. Productivity bought the time. Our World in Data, from Huberman and Minns and the Penn World Table, 2025

    Published tuition and fees at a public four year college rose from $5,940 in 1995 to $11,950 in 2025, after inflation. College Board, Trends in College Pricing, 2025

    For each extra dollar of subsidized federal loans available, colleges raised tuition by about 60 cents. Lucca, Nadauld and Shen, New York Fed Staff Report 733, 2015, revised 2017

    As of 2025, 28 states recognize occupational licenses earned elsewhere. Trudeau, Timmons and Seevers, reported August 2025

    The hard question

    If federal loans shrink, how does a kid without money go to college?

    Today that kid borrows more each year to pay a price the loans helped push up. Capping the loans removes part of the reason tuition rises. Some students will borrow less, or borrow from lenders who look at whether a program pays off. That pushes students away from expensive programs that do not raise earnings and toward cheaper ones that do. Apprenticeships pay the student instead of charging them.

    Your great great grandparents worked 3,096 hours a year. You work 1,789. Keep going.

  2. 2

    Health care you can afford

    See how it works

    Care is expensive because supply is rationed. Let nurses and pharmacists do what they are trained to do. Let new clinics open without asking competitors for permission. Allow safe drugs approved abroad. Post real prices. Own your health savings.

    What is broken

    America spent $5.3 trillion on health care in 2024, about $15,474 per person and 18 percent of the whole economy. That buys a lot of care. The problem is that the rules limit who can provide it and hide what it costs.

    How we fix it

    • Full practice for nurse practitioners and pharmacists, within their training, without paying a doctor to sign off.
    • End certificate of need laws, so a new clinic or scanner no longer needs a state board’s permission that its competitors can contest.
    • Reciprocity: a drug approved by a trusted regulator abroad can be sold here unless the FDA finds a reason to block it.
    • One posted, all in price for every common procedure, before treatment.
    • Larger health savings accounts that follow the person from job to job.

    Posted prices already work. The Surgery Center of Oklahoma lists one all inclusive price per surgery, covering surgeon, anesthesia and facility. A knee arthroscopy is listed at $4,458. surgerycenterok.com price list, 2025

    In Canada’s single payer system, the median wait from a family doctor’s referral to treatment was 28.6 weeks in 2025. Fraser Institute, Waiting Your Turn, 2025

    National health spending: $5.3 trillion, $15,474 per person, 18.0 percent of GDP in 2024, projected to reach 20.6 percent by 2034. CMS National Health Expenditure fact sheet, 2026

    The hard question

    Wouldn’t a single government plan be simpler?

    A single plan sets one budget, and a fixed budget rations care by making people wait. Our approach goes the other way: add providers and make prices visible, so the same money buys far more care. See Who pays.

    A knee scope for $4,458, posted in advance. Why isn’t every price like this?

  3. 3

    Homes everyone can afford

    See how it works

    Rent is high because building is illegal in most places. Allow homes by right. Approve permits on a deadline. End parking mandates. Legalize modular and manufactured homes. More homes push rents down, and nothing else works as well.

    What is broken

    Building a home in America has gotten harder, not easier. Value added per construction worker was about 40 percent lower in 2020 than in 1970, while the rest of the economy got more productive. Before anyone picks up a hammer, a project faces zoning that bans everything but detached houses, discretionary permits that take years, and rules that dictate how many parking spaces a home must come with.

    How we fix it

    • Homes by right: if a project meets the written code, it gets built. No hearing, no veto.
    • Permits on a deadline: a fixed number of days to say yes or no. Silence counts as yes.
    • No parking mandates: builders and buyers decide.
    • Factory built homes approved on the same terms as homes built on site.
    • Lot splits by right, and single stair reform for mid-rise apartments.
    • No rent control: it shrinks the supply it is meant to protect.

    Austin let builders build. The region permitted 957 apartments per 100,000 residents from 2021 to 2023, and rents then fell for 19 straight months, to a typical asking rent of $1,645 in December 2024. Texas Tribune analysis of Census and Zillow data, January 2025

    San Francisco went the other way. Landlords covered by its 1994 rent control expansion cut the rental housing they offered by 15 percent, and the authors conclude the lost supply likely pushed market rents up citywide. Diamond, McQuade and Qian, American Economic Review, 2019

    US construction productivity has been flat or falling for decades. Goolsbee and Syverson, NBER Working Paper 30845, 2023

    The hard question

    New buildings are luxury apartments. How does that help someone who can’t afford them?

    The new units are usually the expensive ones. They help by giving higher earners somewhere new to live, so those people stop bidding up older apartments. Austin’s rents fell across the market, not only in the new towers. It takes a few years, and a neighborhood can change faster than some residents want. A city that blocks new homes still changes. The only difference is that rents rise faster while it does.

    Austin built. Austin’s rents fell 19 months straight.

  4. 4

    Justice that protects you

    See how it works

    Police and courts exist to stop violence, theft and fraud. End qualified immunity so officers answer for abuse. End taking property without a conviction. Treat drug use as a health issue. Put victims, not budgets, at the center of justice.

    How we fix it

    • End qualified immunity: an officer who violates your rights can be sued like anyone else.
    • End civil asset forfeiture without a conviction.
    • End the drug war; treat drug use as a health matter.
    • Restitution to victims at the center of sentencing.
    • Keep police focused on violence, theft and fraud, the core job.

    Where we stand

    Accountable police and courts, because protecting people from violence is the one job government must do.

  5. 5

    Cheap, clean, abundant energy

    See how it works

    Cheap energy makes everything cheaper. Clear the permitting logjam with firm deadlines. License advanced nuclear on a fixed timeline. Connect finished solar and storage to the grid. End subsidies for every fuel. Make polluters pay for real harm.

    What is broken

    About 2,061 gigawatts of proposed generation and storage was waiting for a grid connection at the end of 2025. In the regions with data, projects that make it through take more than five years to reach operation. The projects exist. The rules keep them waiting.

    How we fix it

    • Deadlines for environmental review: two years for a full review, one for a smaller one. Miss the deadline and the project is approved.
    • License proven reactor designs once; each new site gets a yes or no on a set schedule.
    • Projects connect in the order they are ready, and the grid operator carries the cost of delay.
    • No subsidies for any fuel. Oil, gas, coal, solar, wind and nuclear compete on price.
    • A person or town hurt by pollution can collect from whoever caused it.

    A solar module cost $132.38 per watt in 1975 and $0.12 in 2025, both in 2025 dollars, a drop of about 99.9 percent. Prices fall when production is allowed to scale. Our World in Data, from Nemet, Farmer and Lafond, and IRENA, 2025

    The median federal environmental impact statement issued from 2019 to 2024 took 2.8 years from notice to final statement. Council on Environmental Quality, January 2025

    Only three new reactors have started in the US since 2000. Vogtle Units 3 and 4 were planned at $14 billion for 2016 and 2017; they opened in 2023 and 2024 at more than $30 billion. US Energy Information Administration, 2023 and 2024

    The hard question

    Vogtle went over budget and years late. Why bet on nuclear?

    A fixed licensing timeline does not fix bad project management. It does remove the reason no one has built enough plants to get good at it. Builders get cheaper with repetition, and that only happens if the second plant of a design is allowed to start.

    Solar got 99.9 percent cheaper. Now let it plug in.

  6. 6

    Peace

    See how it works

    War is the most expensive thing government does. Defend the country, not the world. Close bases that defend nothing. Make the Pentagon pass an audit before it gets another dollar. Trade with people everywhere; fight only when attacked.

    How we fix it

    • Non-intervention as the default; peace through trade.
    • A military sized to defend the country, not to police the world.
    • Close overseas bases that serve no defensive need.
    • No budget increase until the Pentagon passes a full financial audit.

    Where we stand

    Defense stays a core function of a minimal government. The question is what it defends, and whether it can account for the money.

  7. 7

    Free trade

    See how it works

    Trade between people is how countries get rich and stay at peace. Tariffs are taxes paid by your own families and businesses. Drop broad embargoes that punish ordinary families. Aim any sanctions at named individuals who commit violence, with an end date.

    How we fix it

    • Free trade between people as the default, regardless of what their governments do.
    • Repeal broad tariffs; they are taxes on your own households.
    • Sanctions narrow, targeted at named individuals responsible for violence, with sunset dates.
  8. 8

    A government that can’t be bought

    See how it works

    Lobbyists pay for favors because government sells them. Stop selling. End subsidies, bailouts, special tax breaks and government stakes in private companies. Put an expiration date on every handout. When there is nothing to buy, the buying stops.

    How we fix it

    • A sunset clause on every business subsidy and special tax break.
    • A ban on federal equity stakes in private firms.
    • A pre-committed no-bailout rule.
    • Shrink what government can hand out, and the market for influence shrinks with it.

    Where we stand

    Political speech stays free. The fix is to remove what is for sale.

  9. 9

    Competition, not monopoly

    See how it works

    Big companies stay big when rules protect them from challengers. Strip out the licenses, extended patents and regulations that lock incumbents in. Let failed giants fail. Make it easy for workers to own shares or start cooperatives.

    How we fix it

    • Remove licenses, legally extended patents and regulations that protect incumbents from entry.
    • End bailouts that keep failed giants alive.
    • Make it simple for workers to own shares or form cooperatives, voluntarily.

    The hard question

    Don’t markets create monopolies?

    Most durable monopolies rest on a license, a patent extended by law, or a regulation that blocks entry. The answer is the same every time: let someone compete.

  10. 10

    Workers hold the cards

    See how it works

    Workers have power when employers have to compete for them. End licensing that traps people in one job or one state. Let benefits follow the worker, not the boss. Band together and bargain if you want; nobody is forced to join or pay.

    How we fix it

    • Universal license recognition across states.
    • Portable benefits and health savings that follow the worker.
    • Workers are free to band together and bargain, and employers are free to say no and hire someone else. No government-backed bargaining, no forced membership, no forced dues.
    • A labor market where employers compete for people, because starting and growing a business is easy.

    In 2025, 21.6 percent of employed Americans held a government occupational license. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Current Population Survey, Table 49, 2025

Underneath all ten: free and fair elections, open to every candidate with real support. A free society starts there.

Who pays?

Less than you pay now, and fewer people need help at all.

How does a minimal government fund courts, police and defense?

Courts, police and defense are a small slice of what government spends today, and a small slice is easy to fund. A low, broad tax covers it to start. Loser-pays court rules, private arbitration that competes with public courts, and assurance contracts for shared goods carry more of the load over time.

What about people who cannot pay?

When a home, a clinic visit and a kilowatt hour cost a fraction of what they cost today, far fewer people need help at all. For those who still do, neighbors, charities, mutual aid and fraternal societies carried this load before and will again, with a floor in place while they grow.
